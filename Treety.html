01 / Overview / Street trees / Los Angeles

New street trees die of thirst.Grown ones lift the sidewalk.

Treety is a buried rain box with sand wicks. It catches curb runoff all winter and feeds it to a young tree through the dry season, only as fast as the soil dries. The water arrives 12–18 inches down on the curb side, so that is where the roots grow.

02Problem / new plantings / LA parkways

Two failures with one cause: water in the wrong place.

Thirst

A new street tree needs about 15 gallons a week through the May–October dry season, for three years.3 LA County asks residents to water new parkway trees about twice a week in warm months.5 That is 26 to 52 visits a summer per tree, by a resident, a volunteer, or a watering truck. When the visits stop, young trees die.

Heave

Water poured at the surface keeps roots shallow. Moisture also condenses under concrete, which makes the soil just under the slab the best spot around. Roots there thicken a little every year and push the slab up.

Treety moves the water to where we want the roots: deep, on the curb side, and only when the soil needs it.

How to read it

Tap any number for what that part does and its specs. Blue is stored water. It can't rise above the standpipe rim at 17 in.; anything more drops down the standpipe into the gravel trench. The tan lines are sand wicks: each starts in the reserve at the box floor and rises to an outlet at 14 in., inside the green sweet spot.

As the box drains, the wick climb gets longer: 3 in. when full, about 21 in. near empty. That climb is exactly what the prototype tests.

The year runs from October 1 and uses downtown LA's average monthly rain.2 About 280 gallons reach the box per inch of rain, from roughly 500 sq ft of street. A tan triangle on the chart marks a 150-gallon crew refill, triggered when the box falls to its 25-gallon reserve.

Even a dry winter fills the box, because about 1.4 in. of rain fills it from empty. What decides refills is how long and hot the dry season runs.

04Mechanism / five moves / no pumps, no power

Gravity brings the water in. The soil decides when it comes out.

  1. 1Step 1 / Capture

    Street runoff fills the box

    Gutter water flows through a curb opening onto a grate that catches trash and leaves. Below it, three layers of washed rock, coarse to fine, strip out grit. A flap valve at the box wall lets the clean water in and shuts behind it. About 280 gallons arrive per inch of rain.

    Grate → 1½ in. rock → ¾ in. rock → ⅜ in. pea gravel → flap valve → box

  2. 2Step 2 / Store

    A box the tree can't flood

    Plastic storage crates in a welded liner, 12–36 in. below grade. An adjustable standpipe with its rim at 17 in. caps the water level and sends the extra to a gravel trench, where it soaks down into deep soil. Above the rim is surge space for storms. Flap valves on the inlet and overflow, a gasketed lid, and a screened vent seal it against mosquitoes, as California's mosquito guidance asks for anything that holds water longer than 72 hours.6

    2.5 × 14 ft · overflow 17 in. · about 390 gal · dig 3.3 ft (4 ft at the trench)

  3. 3Step 3 / Release

    Water moves only when the soil pulls

    Six U-shaped sand wicks start in the reserve water at the box floor and rise to outlets 14 in. down. Capillary action lifts water as the soil around each outlet dries, and slows to nothing when it is damp. The outlets sit above the overflow line, so the wicks can't siphon the box empty. The fill is a silt-rich sandy mix: in lab columns, plain fine sand lifted water only about 8 in., while silt lifted it about 5 ft.7

    Tree needs 8–11 L/day · estimate about 3 L/day per 4 in. wick · 6 wicks

  4. 4Step 4 / Steer

    Roots grow where the soil is best

    Roots don't hunt for water. The ones that land in damp, airy soil grow and thicken fastest. Treety puts that soil at 10–20 in. on the curb side. The sidewalk side gets a root barrier and a gravel layer under the slab, which breaks up the damp film that draws roots up. In a long-term field comparison, the barrier and the gravel layer were among the treatments with the least lifting.8 Under the walk, structural soil down to 36 in. carries the pavement and gives roots room to cross deep, toward the yard.9

    Barrier on the sidewalk side only. Never a ring around the tree.

  5. 5Step 5 / Graduate

    Then it steps back

    Roots can't get into the box: the bottom of each U stays underwater, and roots stop at soaked, airless soil. In years 3–5 a crew slides the standpipe down a few inches at a time, so more winter water drains to the gravel trench and deep soil, and the roots follow it down. After year 5 the wick cartridges come out, and the box stays in place as free rain storage.

    Each inch the overflow drops moves about 20 gal from the box to the deep zone

05Long term / depth ladder / years 0–5+

Built to be outgrown.

Treety isn't permanent irrigation. It trains the roots for a few years and then gets out of the way. In most of LA, groundwater sits far below a street tree's roots, so the finish line is a tree that lives on winter rain stored in a deep, roomy soil volume, with the box as backup.10

Sensors at the outlets and in the box tell a crew when to refill and when a tree is ready to move down a rung. Watering deeply and infrequently is what builds a deep, drought-tough root system.11

06Prototype / Rig A / wick bench

Prototype images coming soon.

What we're building

Rig A tests the one number we couldn't find published for this use: how much water a sand wick can lift 24 inches, day after day.

  • Four 2 in. PVC tubes, about 30 in. long, each packed with a different fill: fine sand; 85/15 sand and silica flour; 70/30; sandy loam. One tube is clear so the rising water shows.
  • Each tube stands in a constant-level reservoir (an inverted 2-liter bottle feeding a small tub) and feeds a tray of sandy loam above it.
  • A box fan dries the trays the way a summer dries a parkway. A fifth tray holds plain water, to measure how fast the fan dries things.
  • Each reservoir sits on a kitchen scale, so grams lost per day equals water the wick moved. We test climbs of 24, 18, 12, and 6 in., hardest first.
Pass mark 1.3–1.8 L/day

per field wick, so that six wicks together cover a young tree's 8–11 liters a day. A 2 in. bench tube has about a quarter of the area of a 4 in. field wick, so it passes at about 0.35–0.5 L/day.

The winning fill goes into the full design and into our root simulation. Photos, the time-lapse, and the first flow data will post here as the bench runs.

07Model / data / limits

What we know, and what we're testing.

Proven on its own
  • Deep, infrequent watering grows deeper roots.
  • A root barrier or a gravel layer under the walk had the least sidewalk lifting in a long-term field comparison.
  • Wicking beds keep soil moist from a reservoir below.
  • Curb-fed tree pits capture and clean street runoff.
Still open
  • All of it together in a real LA parkway over several summers.
  • Wick flow at a 24 in. climb. Our 3 L/day estimate assumes the fill stays saturated; Rig A measures it.
  • How fast roots fill the wick legs. Wicks are pull-out cartridges for that reason.
  • Structural soil on its own. In that same comparison it had the most lifting (the differences weren't statistically significant), so Treety only uses it with the barrier and gravel layer.
  • Approvals: a city tree permit, a public works standard plan, and vector control sign-off for the sealed box.
What the year model leaves out
  • Storm timing. Rain is spread evenly through each month, and the first 0.25 in. a month is assumed to wet the pavement without reaching the box.
  • Heat waves. Tree use is fixed: 15 or 20 gal/week May–October, 5 gal/week November–April.
  • Water held in the surrounding soil, an extra buffer on top of the box.
  • Site limits. The gravel trench needs soil that absorbs at least 0.3 in./hr, must sit 10 ft from foundations and property lines, and needs its bottom at least 10 ft above groundwater. Every site starts with a DigAlert utility check.
08Pilot / 2–3 trees / one dry season

Host a pilot tree.

We're looking for a city, a planting nonprofit, or a developer to host two or three Treety trees and monitor them through one dry season, May to October. That summer is the real proof.

CAL FIRE's Urban & Community Forestry grants have funded tree planting, establishment care, and site improvements like bioswales, so Treety can be written into a city or nonprofit's application when the next round opens.12
